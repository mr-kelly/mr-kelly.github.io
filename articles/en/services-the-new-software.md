## Before the essay

I've met a lot of people over the past two years, and I keep seeing an awkward pattern: **the people who know AI are far from the business, and the people who own the business can't keep up with the tools. A layer in the middle is missing.**

MIT's Project NANDA put a number on this gap in its July 2025 report: enterprises have put in $30–40 billion, and 95% of generative AI pilots have produced no measurable P&L impact. The divide isn't caused by model quality. **It's caused by approach.**

So the problem usually isn't “we're not using enough AI.” **Quite the opposite: precisely because the tools are already strong enough, it's now plain to see that nobody is responsible for connecting them to the business.**

So who should pick up that layer? I spent many years in SaaS. Vika (AITable outside China) was one of China's first spreadsheet-database products, and our logic back then was the same as every software company's: build a good tool, hand it to the customer, and the results are the customer's business. Now I doubt that premise still holds. [Foundation Capital](https://foundationcapital.com/ai-service-as-software/), Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella and the Sequoia essay below are all saying the same thing.

The Chinese edition of this piece carries my full translation of the essay. English readers should read the original on Sequoia's site; below is a section-by-section guide, with Sequoia's key lines quoted in their own words.

## The essay: “Services: The New Software”

> Original: [Services: The New Software](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software/), by Julien Bek, Sequoia Capital, published March 5, 2026. Copyright belongs to the author and Sequoia Capital. The summaries below are mine; the quoted lines are Sequoia's.

**“The next $1T company will be a software company masquerading as a services firm.”**

The essay opens with the question every AI tool founder is asking: what happens when the next model turns my product into a feature? Bek's answer: “If you sell the tool, you're in a race against the model.” If you sell the work, every model improvement makes your service faster and cheaper instead. His example: “A company might spend $10K a year for QuickBooks and $120K on an accountant to close the books. The next legendary company will just close the books.”

### Intelligence vs Judgement

“Writing code is mostly *intelligence*. Knowing what to build next is *judgement*.”

Turning specs into code, tests and fixes follows complex rules, but rules all the same. Judgement is different: it rests on experience, taste and intuition built over years, such as what to build next, when to take on tech debt, when to ship before it's ready. Bek points out that software engineering already accounts for more than half of AI tool usage across all professions, with every other category still in single digits, because it is mostly intelligence work. AI can now do most of that work on its own and leave judgement to people. Software got there first; every other profession is next.

### Copilots and Autopilots

“A copilot sells the tool. An autopilot sells the work.”

While models were still maturing, the right move was a copilot: put AI in a professional's hands and let them stay accountable for the output (Harvey for law firms, Rogo for investment banks). Now models are good enough that in some categories the best starting point is an autopilot sold straight to the end customer, who buys the outcome (Crosby for companies that need NDAs drafted, WithCoverage for CFOs buying insurance). In every profession, work budgets dwarf tool budgets, and autopilots draw on the work budget from day one. “The higher the intelligence ratio in any field, the sooner autopilots will win.”

### The Convergence

“Today's judgement will become tomorrow's intelligence.”

As AI systems gather proprietary data on what good judgement looks like in their field, the frontier moves, and copilots and autopilots will eventually converge. The starting point still matters, Bek argues, because it decides where an autopilot can win customers now and begin compounding the data that will later let it handle judgement too.

### The Autopilot Playbook: Outsourcing as the Wedge

“For every dollar spent on software, six are spent on services.”

An autopilot's TAM is all the labor spend in a category, insourced and outsourced. But the right place to start is where outsourcing already exists: the company has accepted that the work can be done outside, a budget line exists to replace, and the buyer is already buying an outcome. In Bek's words: “Replacing an outsourcing contract with an AI-native services provider is a vendor swap. Replacing headcount is a reorg.”

The playbook: start with outsourced, intelligence-heavy tasks, nail distribution, then expand into insourced, judgement-heavy work as AI compounds. “The outsourced task is the wedge. The insourced work is the long-term TAM.” Crosby started with NDAs, a well-defined, intelligence-heavy task most companies already outsource.

### Opportunity Map

Bek plots each services vertical on the intelligence-to-judgement spectrum and by its outsourced-to-insourced ratio, which “produces a priority map with labour TAM in brackets.” He presents the list as illustrative.

![Opportunity map: intelligence vs judgement × outsourced vs insourced｜Image: Sequoia Capital](https://framerusercontent.com/images/WgyUIZzLNSXVHBiyi06RYI2SLE.webp)

*Figure from the original essay. Source: Sequoia Capital, “[Services: The New Software](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/services-the-new-software/)”*

**Insurance brokerage ($140-200B).** Standard commercial lines are highly standardized, and the broker's value is mostly comparing carriers and filling in forms. Distribution is fragmented across thousands of small brokers. New entrants to watch: WithCoverage, Harper.

**Accounting and audit ($50-80B outsourced in the US alone).** A structural shortage of accountants is pushing the profession to adopt AI faster than almost any other. Rillet is building an AI-native ERP; Basis started as a copilot for accountants.

**Healthcare revenue cycle ($50-80B outsourced in US).** Medicine sounds judgement-heavy, but billing is almost pure intelligence: translating clinical notes into ICD-10 codes. Outsourcing is mature and paid by outcome. Anterior is furthest along.

**Claims adjusting ($50-80B including TPAs).** Interpreting policy terms against losses and setting reserves; an aging workforce with no one to replace it; heavily outsourced to independent adjusters and third-party administrators such as Crawford and Sedgwick. Pace is building a claims autopilot; Strala an AI-native TPA.

**Tax advisory ($30-35B).** Licensing is a regulatory moat, but most of the underlying work is intelligence, and each new jurisdiction deepens the data moat. Early players: TaxGPT, and in Europe Skalar and Ravical.

**Legal, transactional work ($20-25B).** Contract drafting, NDAs and regulatory filings are intelligence-heavy, routinely outsourced and standardized enough for quality to be checked. Harvey is pivoting toward autopilot; Crosby and Lawhive are autopilot-native.

**IT managed services ($100B+).** Every small and mid-sized business outsources IT, and the work repeats across thousands of identical environments. Today's software (ConnectWise, Datto) sells tools to the providers; nobody has yet sold “your IT runs” directly to the company as an outcome. Edra automates IT workflows; Serval automates IT support.

**Supply chain and procurement ($200B+).** Most companies only negotiate seriously with their top suppliers; the long tail goes unmanaged, and contract leakage costs a few percent of spend. The wedge is abandoned work, which is found money. Magentic (direct procurement), AskLio (indirect) and Tacto (mid-market) are in play.

**Recruitment and staffing ($200B+).** The top of the funnel is pure intelligence; closing candidates and judging culture fit is judgement. The autopilot wedge is high-volume, low-judgement roles. Juicebox, Mercor and Jack & Jill are emerging leaders.

**Management consulting ($300-400B).** A huge market, but mostly judgement. The open question is whether AI can split consulting into an intelligence layer to automate and a judgement layer left to people. Best candidate: to be determined.

The essay closes on timing. The fastest-growing AI companies of 2025 were copilots, and many will try to become autopilots in 2026. “But they also face the innovator's dilemma: selling the work means cutting their own customers out of doing it. That's the opening for pure-play autopilots.”

## After the essay

This essay is written for founders: which company to build, which category to enter. From the buyer's side, it's useful in a different way: **it's a manual for how other people plan to take your budget.**

Three notes.

**First, discount it for China.** The essay's wedge, “already outsourced means replaceable,” assumes a mature outsourcing market. In China, outsourcing of audit, transactional legal work, insurance brokerage and IT management is far less common than in the US. What you're up against is often not a contract you can swap out but a few people sitting in your office. In the essay's own terms, the first is a vendor swap; the second is a reorg.

**Second, the two-way split is missing a category.** Beyond intelligence and judgement, a lot of work inside Chinese companies is **coordination**: getting people aligned, agreeing on one version of the numbers, untangling cross-department dependencies. AI can't take that on today, and it fills more than half the time in many roles. If you estimate how much AI can replace by the intelligence share alone, you'll overestimate every time.

**Third, the thing none of the three says.** The middle layer stays broken not only because nobody comes to pick it up, but because **whoever comes to pick it up can't get paid.** Selling by outcome only works when a third party can verify the outcome. If your acceptance criteria amount to “make it a bit better,” any rational vendor will only sell you tools and person-days.

> **AI results = Use-case choice × Owner × Acceptance criteria**

**Defining “done” is the hardest thing to automate in this era.**
