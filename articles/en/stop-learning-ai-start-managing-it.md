Recently, a discussion about “how to learn AI” made me rethink a very common problem.

Picture someone like this.

It's not that they don't understand AI. They use Doubao, DeepSeek and ChatGPT (Doubao is ByteDance's chatbot, one of the most popular in China), and they've had AI write articles, make slide decks, tidy up meeting notes and draft first versions of proposals. They've bought two or three AI courses, saved hundreds of prompts, and have more than one Agent product on their phone.

But a year on, they aren't working fewer hours.

Sometimes they work longer, because now, for every task, they first have to decide “can AI do this?”, then explain the background, wait for the result, and then check it, fix it, move it and publish it themselves.

I asked them three questions:

**Last week, was there a single job AI did from start to finish, where you only confirmed it once?**

**If you were offline all day tomorrow, which piece of work would keep moving?**

**When it's done, who judges that it's right, and by what standard?**

If you can't answer any of the three, what you're missing probably isn't more AI knowledge.

What's missing is this: **you've never actually handed a job over completely.**

I call this state **the super-worker trap.**

The stronger AI gets, the busier you get.

Because every piece of output still has to pass through one bottleneck: you. You haven't gained a team. You've just upgraded yourself into a more efficient, more exhausted super-worker.

Why does this happen?

I put it into a very simple formula:

> **AI output = Capability × Authority × Acceptance**

Note that it's multiplication, not addition.

Over the past three years, almost everyone has put their effort into the first factor: learning prompts, comparing models, chasing new tools, signing up for new courses.

But today the first factor is no longer the bottleneck. **It's the only one of the three that isn't in short supply.**

Models can write code, read documents, operate a browser and complete dozens of steps in a row. On many specific tasks, their capability already exceeds what the job demands day to day.

What's really stuck is the other two.

You haven't handed a job over completely, so authority is 0.

You haven't defined what counts as done or who signs off, so acceptance is 0.

If any factor is 0, the product is 0.

So for most people, the problem isn't “I haven't learned AI yet.”

**It's the opposite: because you keep learning, you never hand the work over. Endless learning is itself a refusal to delegate.**

Learning feels like progress. Delegating feels like losing control.

So again and again, we pick whichever feels better.

## I. The way people learn AI was the wrong paradigm from the start

Our generation learned every tool the same way: learn the features, learn the shortcuts, learn the best practices, practice until fluent, and then do the work faster yourself.

That's how it went with Excel, with Photoshop, with CapCut.

This method rests on an assumption nobody ever says out loud: **the tool doesn't think; the ceiling on capability is you.** The better you learn it, the better its output.

AI is the first thing to break that assumption.

Its capability doesn't live in you, and on many specific tasks it already exceeds yours.

When what you're facing is an executor more capable than you, “learning how to use it” is the wrong goal.

Change the setting and you'll see it right away.

You don't treat a new hire the way you treat a tool.

You wouldn't spend three months studying “how to use Sam properly” before deciding whether to give them any work.

What you do is give them a role, give them the materials they need, explain the process, and check their work at the key points.

**What you do with a new hire is management, not learning.**

AI has crossed that line. What it needs is management too.

This also explains why so many people get more anxious the more they learn: the tools update every week, the prompts you memorized yesterday stop working today, and you're speeding up on a track you can never catch up on.

Management doesn't go out of date.

**Roles, materials, process and acceptance: anyone who led a team thirty years ago would recognize them.**

## II. Being able to execute isn't the same as being able to manage

I came at this from the wrong side myself.

Before Agent products appeared, I thought I was already very good at using AI.

I'd take a foldable phone and a small keyboard to a Hong Kong-style café, connect remotely to my computer at home, and keep ten terminals open on screen at once, with different AIs researching, writing code and handling tasks for me.

From the outside it looked cool, as if I were one person leading a whole team.

But look closely at each window: I was the one starting it, I was the one following up, I was the one checking, I was the one deciding the next step.

More AI, and I was still the project manager, the coordinator and the courier.

What really changed my mind was an accident.

That Chinese New Year, at the dinner table, I had a thought: since it can already operate my computer, could it go through my contacts and prepare a batch of New Year greetings? I handed the task over from my phone.

I only said what I wanted done.

I didn't give it filtering criteria, I didn't set a cap on the number, and I didn't say whether it should show me before sending.

It carried out the task very conscientiously.

In the end it sent nine thousand messages, and it took two days and two nights.

The story sounds funny, but it was my first lesson in managing AI:

**An employee who can execute doesn't automatically become a good employee without boundaries.**

From then on, I stopped treating “fully automatic” as the goal.

The real goal is this: AI reliably handles the repetitive, low-risk steps; anywhere that touches contacts, public posts, pricing, contracts, payments or deletion, it must stop and have a person confirm.

**The more capable the AI, the more it needs a clear role, materials, process and acceptance.**

## III. Running an AI company comes down to four things

“Running an AI company” sounds big. Broken down, it's just four things.

### 1. Role: what it's responsible for, and what it isn't

Stop giving AI tasks. Give it a responsibility.

“Write me a WeChat Official Account post” is a task. Once it's used, it's gone.

“You own the WeChat Official Account content: from source material to topic, first draft, images and into the drafts folder; I confirm before anything is published” is a role. It can keep existing.

The most important line in a job description is often not what it's responsible for, but **what it isn't responsible for, and when it must stop.**

### 2. Materials: which facts and standards it works from

When a new hire does poorly, it's usually not because they're slow. It's because nobody told them how the company actually works.

AI is exactly the same.

What it needs isn't a longer prompt; it's your real business materials: how the product is priced, what customers often ask, what must never be said, and what three past examples of good work look like.

**Prompts are wording; materials are facts.** Most people get stuck here because preparing materials is far more tedious than tweaking prompts.

### 3. Process: how one success becomes repeatable

Plenty of people have had a “wow, AI did great this time” moment, and then nothing came of it.

Because that success only ever lived in one conversation.

The process has to answer: where does the source material come in, which steps does it pass through, where does the output go, when is it handed to a person, and after the person deals with it, which step does it go back to?

One success isn't a capability. **Only what can be run again and again is a capability.**

### 4. Acceptance: where people judge, approve and handle exceptions

This is the most often skipped, and the most expensive.

If you don't define acceptance in advance, you can only judge by gut feeling afterwards whether it's good, then fix it yourself, and the work lands back on you.

Back to the formula: **the first two things determine authority; the last two determine acceptance.** Only with all four in place is the product not 0.

## IV. How one AI grows into a company

I've walked this road myself. Looking back, it's just five steps.

**Step one: you talk to AI.** You ask, it answers, and you explain from scratch every time.

**Step two: you hand over one complete task.** Start to finish, without stepping in midway.

**Step three: it owns a fixed role.** No longer a one-off job, but a patch of work that stays its responsibility.

**Step four: different roles start handing off to each other.** The content role passes drafts to review, the leads role passes candidates to sales, the email role leaves the few high-risk emails for you.

**Step five: you're managing a company, not dozens of chat windows.**

After Chinese New Year, I started giving different Agents different roles: some watched engineering tickets, some sorted email, some made content, some found leads.

Two days later, the company had thirty roles like this.

The number isn't the point. The real change was that they started to have roles, business materials, upstream and downstream, and to know when they had to come back to a person.

Thirty roles also brought new trouble: who's doing what? Which materials did they use? Who did the results go to? Where is something waiting for my approval?

That's why we went on to build Buda. Not to make another chat box, but to give this company a workbench, so that roles, materials, task status, deliverables and approval points can all be seen.

Today, a more useful question than “how many AI tools have you learned?” is:

**Which step are you on?**

## V. Week one: how to actually hand over your first job

Don't start by designing a whole company.

Hand over one job first. There are only four criteria for choosing it: **frequent, low-risk, clearly bounded, and you can tell at a glance whether the result is right.**

First drafts of content, organizing materials, sorting customer questions, compiling daily and weekly reports, and first-pass lead screening usually all fit.

Anything involving payments, contracts, commitments to outsiders or deleting data: don't touch it in week one.

- **Day 1: write the job description.** Half a page is enough: what it's responsible for, what it isn't, and which three situations mean it must stop and come to me.
- **Days 2–3: prepare the materials.** Put in the facts and standards this job really depends on, plus three past examples of good work. This step is the most tedious, and it decides success or failure more than any other.
- **Day 4: run it end to end once, without stepping in.** Let it go all the way through. Only note where it goes wrong; don't quietly fix things for it.
- **Day 5: define acceptance.** Write down “what counts as done,” and which point must have your nod.
- **Days 6–7: have it do the same job again.** This time, what you're watching isn't quality; it's consistency.

After a week, the conclusion you want isn't “I've learned it.”

It's this: **this job no longer goes through me.**

## VI. What counts as done: a delivery standard you can copy

What a role hands back can't be just the result.

It should include at least seven things:

- **Problem**: what this run is meant to solve;
- **Basis**: which materials and facts it used;
- **Actions**: what exactly it did;
- **Uncertainties**: where it isn't sure and needs a person to judge;
- **Evidence**: screenshots, links or source text, so a person can double-check;
- **Risks**: what still isn't covered;
- **Sign-off**: who it's waiting on for approval, and whether it may be published.

With these seven, your confirmation goes from “redo the whole thing” to “take a look, then approve or send it back.”

**AI makes the process transparent. People make the call on the result.**

That line can't be reversed.

## VII. Three things I don't promise

At this point, I want to be clear about the limits.

**First, I don't promise full automation.** Where contacts, public posts, pricing, contracts, payments and deletion are involved, a person has to be there. Everyone who chases full automation ends up paying tuition like those nine thousand messages.

**Second, I don't promise to replace a certain number of people.** How an organization changes should be decided by looking at the facts once the work is transparent, not an answer written before the project begins. Make layoffs the goal from the start, and the first thing the team learns isn't how to use AI; it's how to hide problems.

**Third, I don't promise any one tool will work forever.** The products that go by these names today may be replaced by a new batch next year. But roles, materials, process and acceptance move over as a set. That's why I insist on teaching methods, not buttons.

## VIII. If you want to start, where to come in

Different people are stuck in different places, so there shouldn't be only one way in.

**If you're an individual, a freelancer or a one-person company**: start with the courses and the community, build your first role on real work, then add a second and a third. This path doesn't require you to be technical.

**If you're a company whose leadership believes in AI, but priorities are scattered and nothing moves across departments**: you need to bring in a CAIO Office (an outside Chief AI Officer team that works with you month by month), with a CAIO Lead who keeps setting priorities and aligns tasks and acceptance.

**If you already have a clear use case and need it truly wired into your systems, data and permissions**: that's the work of a Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE), who goes on site, connects the Agents, data, processes, testing and launch, and finally hands the method back to your team.

**If your company already has a lot of AI output, but it's scattered everywhere**: first bring the data, knowledge, Agent skills and applications together in one place and govern them, then talk about scaling.

I don't ask anyone to buy a particular product first. A tool only becomes part of the delivery when it fits the goal at hand.
