Imagine a company that gives everyone access to AI.

Salespeople write proposals faster. Product managers write requirements faster. Engineers write code faster, too. Yet a piece of work still passes through the same meetings, forwarded messages, queues and approvals on its way from request to completion.

Everyone feels faster. The company as a whole struggles to say where it has actually gained speed.

To understand that gap, I want to take you into a factory more than a century ago.

In many factories with a centralized source of power, a waterwheel or steam engine sent power to individual machines through shafts, gears and belts. Where to place the machines, and how to construct the building, depended on how that power could reach them.

In other words, the production process had to accommodate the shape of the power system.

When electric motors arrived, some factories took a perfectly natural first step: replace the original power source and keep driving the existing transmission system.

Were there benefits? Yes. Power control and energy use, among other things, could improve. But the machines still followed the old transmission layout. Materials still traveled along the old routes.

**A new power source. Work still organized around the old drive shaft.**

Later, as group drive and individual electric drive developed, machines could be positioned more flexibly and started or stopped independently. Factory buildings offered greater freedom to arrange production around the sequence of operations and the flow of materials. This was a key change in the evolution of electrification studied by economic historian Warren Devine. [Research on the history of electrification](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1983-devine.pdf)

It would be a mistake to read this transition as evidence that electricity had no value. Quite the opposite: electricity had opened up new possibilities. The old production structure had yet to make full use of them.

Today, when we insert increasingly capable AI into an entirely unchanged workflow, that scene has a familiar echo.

**Technology can advance before organizations learn how to work with it.**

That distance is where history begins to rhyme.

Let's turn the clock back a little further.

In the middle of the fifteenth century, Gutenberg developed a mature system of metal movable-type printing in Europe. This refers specifically to the European technological path: China and Korea had important printing and movable-type traditions much earlier.

One revealing detail is that early printed books did not immediately look like the books we know today. Some surviving copies of the Gutenberg Bible still have hand-painted decoration, carrying on the appearance of manuscripts. [Library of Congress collection guide](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg/resources)

This is easy to understand. The first buyers knew manuscripts. Familiar layouts and decoration were already part of how they judged a book.

But once texts could be reproduced in batches, the changes began to reach beyond making an existing book faster. Publishing, distribution, readership and the exchange of knowledge would gradually evolve around the new conditions of reproduction. Access to the same text, and the ability to compare and cite it, began to operate under different conditions. [The Gutenberg Bible and the circulation of texts](https://guides.loc.gov/gutenberg/introduction)

Look at today's AI from that perspective, and a habit worth questioning comes into view: our first instinct is often to reproduce documents that already exist.

People used to write weekly reports. Now we ask AI to write them. People used to prepare presentation materials. Now we ask AI to prepare them.

These are useful applications. But if producing text keeps getting cheaper, we will eventually have to answer another question: how should the arrangements through which we use documents to pass on information and coordinate action change?

Move forward to the Industrial Revolution, and the story takes a different form.

We tend to join the steam engine and the factory system into a single sentence, as though modern factories followed directly from Watt's improvements to the steam engine. The actual history is more complicated. Early British textile factories relied heavily on waterpower, and the factory system developed before steam power became widespread. Steam power subsequently expanded the choice of factory locations and helped factories develop in cities. [US National Park Service: The Industrial Revolution in England](https://home.nps.gov/lowe/learn/photosmultimedia/industry.htm)

Machines, power, buildings, workers and markets evolved together in this process.

The shift from dispersed household production to centralized production also required people to work out common hours, coordination between operations, equipment maintenance and production discipline. More powerful machinery solved only part of the problem.

Here is a fact that is often passed over: **the way production works must itself be invented.**

In hindsight, we call it the Industrial Revolution. Those living through it had to deal with specific problems, one by one: where a machine should go, who would operate it, when one operation should hand work to the next, and who would take responsibility when something went wrong.

In the computer age, people noticed a similar gap again.

In 1990, economic historian Paul David published “The Dynamo and the Computer,” directly comparing the history of electrification with the productivity puzzle of the computer age. He pointed to a striking similarity: old factories added electric motors while retaining shafts and belts; companies installed computers while retaining paper records and procedures. [David's original paper](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1990-david.pdf)

The new system went live. The old system kept running.

A company could therefore own plenty of computers and still pass information around as it had in the paper era. People produced forms faster, then handed them to someone else to enter the information all over again.

Research at the firm level has also found complementarities among information technology, changes in workplace organization, and new products and services. The returns to technology are related to how a company allocates tasks, authority and people. [Research on IT and organizational complementarities](https://inequality.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/media/_media/pdf/Reference%20Media/Bresnahan_Brynjolfsson_Hitt_2002_Organizations.pdf)

The internet extended this question across company boundaries.

Moving a brochure onto a website was an application that people could readily understand. But network connections could also enable transactions between people who previously could not find one another, while allowing inventory, orders and fulfillment to be reorganized around new ways of connecting.

In 1995, AuctionWeb, which later became eBay, went online. It organized transactions between buyers and sellers. The platform subsequently developed supporting mechanisms such as transaction feedback. [eBay's official history](https://www.ebayinc.com/company/our-history/)

As connecting became easier, new difficulties came into view: how strangers could establish trust, pay, deliver and resolve disputes.

That is why there is so much work between building a website and building an internet business.

The pattern becomes clearer when we place these changes side by side. The table below compares representative paths and mechanisms. The pace and sequence of development differed across places and industries. The AI row includes both practices already emerging and directions that still need to be tested.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Printing, the European path from the fifteenth century | Reproducing texts became easier to scale | Using a new process to preserve the content and appearance of manuscripts | Publishing, distribution, reading and the exchange of knowledge | Once documents can be generated in batches, how information travels also needs a rethink |

What keeps recurring in this table is a process: a capability that used to be expensive becomes cheaper, creating the possibility of redesigning the production arrangements built around it.

But those old arrangements do not automatically disappear when technology advances.

This is the first key to understanding where AI stands today.

Old factories had good reasons to place their machines around drive shafts. Move a machine too far away, and power might not reach it. Similarly, a company today may have complicated handoffs and approvals to address real problems: incomplete information, inconsistent quality and authority that cannot be handed out casually.

Old structures often embody past experience. Buildings, equipment, skills, performance measures and responsibilities have already formed around them. Changing one part requires considering whether the rest can keep up.

So the coexistence of old and new systems usually has economic reasons. In discussing the spread of electrification, David also considered electricity prices, the replacement of old factory buildings and engineering experience. [David's analysis of technological diffusion](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/automation/1990-david.pdf)

Understanding this keeps us from reducing a technological transition to a simple story in which smart people embrace change and conservative people reject the future.

The real difficulty is that the old system still has value while the new one has already opened up fresh possibilities. You have to learn how to change while operations continue.

The second key is to look at where the bottleneck has moved.

Suppose a proposal must pass through research, drafting, fact-checking, review by the person responsible and coordination of execution before it can be accepted. AI might dramatically shorten the drafting time, while review and coordination continue to hold up the work as a whole.

If the number of proposals generated upstream keeps rising, the queue on that person's desk could even grow longer.

**A local speed gain can make the next bottleneck more congested.**

There is now a concrete example of this.

On October 13, 2026, Anthropic disclosed that, as it made extensive use of agents, its software development bottlenecks had shifted to version control, builds and continuous integration designed for the scale of human work. Continuous integration means bringing code changes together and automatically checking whether they work. To address this, the company rebuilt its underlying version control system. [Anthropic's engineering account](https://www.anthropic.com/research/redesigning-source-control)

This is an AI vendor describing its own unusual working environment. It cannot be treated as representative of every company. But it offers an illuminating scene: as the capacity to write code expands, the systems that receive that code must change too.

More than a century ago, people had to rethink where machines should stand.

Today, they are beginning to rethink how code is merged, how results are checked and how work is handed over.

From this perspective, the historical position of agents becomes easier to understand.

Here, an agent means an AI system that can use tools in pursuit of a goal and decide what to do next based on feedback from its actions. Within a defined scope of authority, it can keep moving a task forward while returning decisions to people when necessary. [How agents work](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents)

For example, an ordinary question-and-answer interaction can tell you how to analyze a dataset. An agent connected to the right tools can read files, run the analysis, check the results and assemble the deliverables. What it can actually achieve depends on the model, tools, data and task itself.

This takes AI beyond providing an answer and into participating in a stretch of work.

In discussions about putting AI to work, I have consistently used this framework:

> **AI output = Capability × Authority × Acceptance**

It is a framework for checking the conditions under which work happens, not a mathematical model for calculating economic growth. Capability determines what it can do. Authority determines how far it may proceed. Acceptance determines which results can be accepted.

When capability expands quickly, gaps in the other two become more visible.

An agent may be able to perform an analysis but lack access to the necessary material. It may be able to prepare a change but not know under what conditions it may submit it. It may generate plenty of results but lack feedback that can identify errors. People then return to the middle of the workflow, moving information, adding judgments and initiating each next step.

The person who stays in the middle coordinating all of this begins to resemble that old drive shaft.

Their experience may remain irreplaceable. But it is worth examining which parts of the work truly require their judgment, and which pass through them simply because the systems are not connected.

If the latter can be reduced, the changes agents bring can gradually extend into how work is organized, how tasks are divided and how teams collaborate.

At this point, though, we cannot draw an overly comfortable conclusion: reorganize the process, and AI's benefits are guaranteed to follow.

The history of the electric motor cannot establish the reliability of an agent.

AI can produce plausible errors and drift away from its goal over a sequence of actions. More authority therefore needs corresponding verification, execution records, exception handling by people and clear arrangements for responsibility.

In that sense, the similarities in technological history also reveal what is different about this revolution: we are trying to reorganize work that contains judgment and uncertainty.

Where results are easier to check, AI can make repeated attempts based on feedback. For example, whether a program passes tests generally offers some evidence sooner than whether a long-term business judgment is correct. Even then, passing tests does not mean that every business objective has been met.

So, to assess the progress of agents, we need to look beyond stronger models and ask whether people can connect goals, execution, feedback and responsibility into a reliable process.

The meaning of completion also needs to become clearer. Take fixing a software defect. Changing the code is just an action. A delivery that can be accepted requires the original problem to be reproducible, the problem to disappear after the change, related functions to pass checks, and the person responsible to approve deployment based on the records.

That is also why I will not promise a fixed efficiency multiplier on the strength of a demonstration. Capabilities shown in a demo must withstand a real environment before they become business returns.

There is another layer to consider: technological progress changes which work is worth doing.

When a task is expensive, we consciously forgo many possibilities. We serve only customers large enough to justify the effort, test only the most important proposals and address only the most urgent problems. Sometimes these choices reflect limits of capability. Sometimes they reflect limits of cost.

If agents can reduce the total cost of a type of work, including execution, checking and handling errors, work that was not previously worth doing may become worthwhile.

Assessing their impact therefore also means looking for new products, a broader range of services and work that was previously unaffordable. These possibilities need to be tested against real demand. Generating more does not, by itself, prove that more value has been created.

From this perspective, technological revolutions also keep changing what is scarce. As copying becomes easier, distribution and selection matter more. As connecting becomes easier, trust and fulfillment matter more. As some forms of cognition and execution become cheaper, we have to reconsider where the problem lies, which results are worth pursuing and who is willing to pay for them.

The third key is to recognize two different speeds at once.

Models and software can update quickly. Organizational learning, changes in authority, business experiments and the development of trust each have their own pace.

Research on the productivity J-curve discusses a related mechanism: new technologies require complementary investments in processes, products and skills. Many are intangible assets that are difficult to measure fully, so early investment and later returns may appear at different times in the statistics. [Research on the productivity J-curve](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20180386)

But the J-curve does not promise that every failed project will succeed if given enough time. Some investments accumulate useful capabilities. Others simply consume resources repeatedly. Evidence is still needed to tell them apart.

This also means it is difficult to assign today's AI a single date on a historical timeline.

Within the same company, one team may still be experimenting with AI to write documents. Another may already have agents carrying out clearly defined tasks. A third may be starting to change the entire process of development, checking and delivery. These stages can coexist.

If we treat history as a precise timetable, we may assume that AI will take as long as electricity did, or that each kind of winner in the past must have a matching company today.

History offers no such guarantee.

What it offers is a more useful set of questions. Which costs does the new capability reduce? What constraints did the existing process address? Where has the bottleneck moved as capability increases? What complementary conditions are still missing before the results can actually be used?

Follow these questions, and the next step for AI extends beyond the next model launch.

It also lies in a task that no longer needs to be passed back and forth, a checking mechanism that catches errors in time, and a way of collaborating that lets people concentrate on the judgments that matter.

History rhymes. Whenever a new capability enters production, people have to learn again how to organize it into valuable work.

**New technology enters old processes, and work speeds up.**

**When people reorganize processes around new capabilities, production begins to change.**

You can start by observing a piece of work you know well. From the moment it is requested to the moment it is completed, how much time goes into execution, and how much into waiting, handoffs and reconfirmation? Then examine the evidence it leaves behind: the original problem, the assessment of its cause, the specific changes, verification results, before-and-after images, remaining risks and who ultimately accepts the work.

Somewhere in there, you may find your company's old drive shaft.
